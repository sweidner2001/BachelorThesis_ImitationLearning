\subsection{Representation Learning}
\textbf{Goal:} Representation Learning aims to extract meaningful and task-relevant spatio-temporal features from large amounts of unlabeled visual data. In robotics and Imitation Learning, the goal is to learn representations that enable agents to interpret dynamic scenes, understand interactions, and generalize across varying environments and embodiments. 

A key challenge is that robotic observations are high-dimensional and subject to variations such as viewpoint changes, lighting conditions, cluttered backgrounds, and partial occlusions. Therefore, learned representations should be invariant to irrelevant visual changes while preserving information relevant for control and decision making.

Modern representation learning methods focus on several key aspects:

    \item \textbf{Spatial Understanding:} Capturing objects, scene layouts, semantic structures or patterns, and relationships between entities.
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal Modeling:} Understanding motion dynamics, temporal coherence, and action progression across video frames [35, 36, 37, 38, 39].
    
    \item \textbf{Spatio-Temporal Modeling:} Extracting features within a frame (spatial) and tracking changes across frames (temporal coherence and dynamics) [35, 39, 44].

This capability is particularly relevant for robotic manipulation tasks, where accurate spatial reasoning is a prerequisite for grasp planning, obstacle avoidance, and trajectory optimization.
    
    \item \textbf{Robustness and Invariance:} Learning consistent representations across multiple views, lighting conditions, and environmental variations [35, 36, 44].

    \textit{Analogy:} This is the motion and dynamics of the video—understanding that a hand is moving toward a block, or that the block is rotating.

Video-centric representation learning is particularly important in robotics because it explicitly models temporal dynamics and multi-view consistency [35, 36, 39, 44]. These learned representations form the backbone of vision-based perception and control systems, enabling the transfer of high-level behavioral information from human videos to robotic policies [45, 46, 47]. As a result, strong representations can significantly improve generalization, robustness, and data efficiency in imitation learning systems or facilitate more robust sim-to-real transfer.






Theoretical Context
The ability to extract meaningful spatiotemporal features from unlabeled visual data constitutes one of the foundational challenges in modern computer vision and robot learning. Representation learning, as a subfield of machine learning, is concerned with the automatic discovery of data representations that are suitable for downstream tasks such as classification, control, or planning (Bengio et al., 2013). In the context of video-conditioned policy learning, the quality of learned visual representations directly determines whether a robotic agent can successfully interpret dynamic environments and act accordingly.


Spatio-Temporal Modeling
A central requirement for video-based representation learning is the capacity to model both spatial and temporal dimensions of visual input simultaneously. On the spatial axis, learned representations must capture structural patterns within individual frames — including object identities, spatial relationships between entities, and scene semantics. On the temporal axis, representations must encode motion dynamics, state transitions, and temporal coherence across consecutive frames. This dual requirement distinguishes video representation learning from its image-based counterpart, as the temporal dimension introduces additional complexity related to motion estimation, occlusion handling, and long-range temporal dependencies. Approaches such as 3D convolutional networks (Tran et al., 2015) and Video Transformers (Arnab et al., 2021) have been proposed to jointly capture these spatio-temporal structures within unified architectures.


Geometry-Aware Representations
Beyond surface-level appearance features, a robust visual encoder must develop an implicit or explicit understanding of three-dimensional scene geometry. Geometry-aware representations enable the inference of depth, object scale, and spatial configuration from monocular or multi-view inputs. This capability is particularly relevant for robotic manipulation tasks, where accurate spatial reasoning is a prerequisite for grasp planning, obstacle avoidance, and trajectory optimization. Recent work has demonstrated that self-supervised depth estimation (Godard et al., 2019) and neural radiance fields (Mildenhall et al., 2020) can serve as effective geometric priors, enriching the feature space available to downstream policy networks.


Perceptual Consistency and Invariance
A further desirable property of visual representations is robustness to variations that are irrelevant to the underlying task. Specifically, learned features should exhibit invariance to changes in viewpoint, illumination conditions, and visual clutter — a property commonly referred to as perceptual consistency. Without such invariance, a policy trained on demonstrations from one visual perspective may fail catastrophically when deployed under slightly different conditions. Contrastive learning frameworks (Chen et al., 2020) and multi-view consistency objectives (Sermanet et al., 2018) have proven effective at encouraging representations that remain stable across such nuisance variations, thereby facilitating more robust sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization.

Self-Supervised Learning Paradigms
A defining characteristic of video-based representation learning is its reliance on self-supervised learning paradigms, which generate training signals from the data itself rather than requiring manual annotations. Three principal approaches have emerged in the literature. First, (time-)contrastive learning constructs positive and negative pairs based on temporal proximity, encouraging the encoder to produce similar embeddings for temporally adjacent frames and dissimilar embeddings for distant ones (Sermanet et al., 2018). Second, visual tracking methods leverage object persistence across frames as a supervisory signal, learning representations that maintain consistent object identity over time. Third, masked autoencoding (MAE) approaches (He et al., 2022) randomly mask portions of the input and train the encoder to reconstruct the missing content, thereby forcing the model to develop rich internal representations of visual structure. These paradigms are not mutually exclusive and can be combined to yield complementary feature spaces.

Relevance to Policy Learning
The representations produced through these methods serve as the perceptual backbone for downstream visuomotor control pipelines. Rather than learning task-specific visual features from scratch — which would require prohibitively large amounts of task-specific demonstration data — pre-trained visual encoders provide a compact, informative feature space upon which policy networks can be efficiently trained. This is particularly advantageous in the context of learning from human demonstration videos, where the visual domain differs substantially from the robot's embodiment. High-quality representations enable the extraction of task-relevant semantic features (e.g., object states, contact events, goal configurations) that transfer across the human-to-robot domain gap, thereby supporting high-level feature transfer from human videos to robot policies.

Approaches and Generalization
Current approaches to video representation learning can be broadly categorized into video-centric methods, which operate exclusively on video sequences and exploit temporal structure, and hybrid video-and-image methods, which additionally leverage large-scale image datasets to improve spatial feature quality. The capacity for generalization -- the ability of learned representations to transfer to novel objects, scenes, and task configurations not encountered during pre-training -- remains a critical evaluation criterion and an active area of research.

Key Takeaways
Spatio-temporal duality is the defining challenge: representations must simultaneously capture frame-level spatial structure and cross-frame motion dynamics.
Geometry awareness and perceptual invariance are essential for robotic applications where viewpoint and lighting conditions vary between training and deployment.
Self-supervised objectives (contrastive learning, tracking, MAE) eliminate the need for labeled data, making large-scale video pre-training feasible.
Transfer quality of learned representations determines how efficiently downstream policies can be trained, particularly when bridging the human-to-robot domain gap.

These approaches established that hand motion and contact patterns provide strong signals about object functionality and affordances.

Building on this idea, more recent methods explicitly incorporate hand information into affordance learning pipelines. For example, hand-aided affordance grounding approaches such as HAG-Net [49] use hand cues from demonstration videos to improve the localization of functional object regions. This reflects a shift toward more fine-grained, interaction-aware affordance prediction, where hand-object contact serves as a key supervisory signal.

Beyond purely spatial reasoning, subsequent work extends affordance learning toward functional and contextual understanding. Generative models and structured probabilistic approaches [50,51] integrate spatial context, human intention, and sub-activity structure to reason about affordances in a more holistic manner. These methods move beyond static object representations by explicitly modeling dependencies between object states, actions, and temporal interaction structure.

A further line of work focuses on learning from demonstration (LfD) videos, where affordances are inferred from curated or weakly labeled human demonstrations. Methods such as Demo2Vec [52] learn embedding spaces that capture interaction semantics, enabling prediction of interaction regions and supporting both robotic and human-centric understanding. In contrast, Vision-Robotics Bridge (VRB) [53] emphasizes learning agent-agnostic affordance representations from large-scale, in-the-wild videos, thereby improving transferability to robotic systems.

In parallel, multi-modal and geometry-aware approaches have significantly advanced affordance perception. RGB-D based models such as AffordanceNet [54] perform joint object and affordance segmentation, enabling pixel-wise functional labeling of object regions. Complementary studies [56] demonstrate that incorporating 3D point cloud representations and multi-view consistency improves spatial reasoning and reduces uncertainty in manipulation tasks.

Finally, more recent methods expand the scope from static affordance prediction to explicit modeling of human-object interaction and manipulation processes. Transformer-based architectures [57] and egocentric vision approaches [58] enable joint spatial-temporal reasoning over actions and object states, while large-scale datasets such as H2O [59] provide synchronized multi-view RGB-D data for two-handed manipulation. Additional works further bridge the gap between perception and control by modeling hand-object contact consistency for grasp generation [60], jointly predicting 3D hand-object poses and action categories [61], and learning weakly supervised segmentation of hands and manipulated objects from motion cues [62]. Collectively, these approaches highlight the increasing integration of spatial, temporal, and geometric reasoning in affordance learning for robotic manipulation.

\subsection{Human Action \& Activity Understanding}
\textbf{Goal:} Human Action and Activity Understanding aims to recognize and structure human behavior in videos, which is essential for grounding object affordances and enabling robotic imitation learning.

Modern methods focus on several key aspects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temporal Action Segmentation and Structure:} Modeling the temporal structure of activities by decomposing long video sequences into meaningful sub-actions or atomic interaction steps, enabling robots to reason over task progression and execution order [68, 69].

    \item \textbf{Fine-Grained Action Recognition:} Distinguishing subtle manipulation differences (e.g., grasping vs. pinching, rotating vs. twisting) by jointly leveraging appearance and motion cues, often through deep convolutional or hybrid architectures [66, 67].

    \item \textbf{Interaction and Motion Modeling:} Predicting interaction regions, motion trajectories, and keypoints to capture how humans interact with objects over time, providing explicit signals for manipulation understanding and imitation [68].

    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Activity Modeling:} Learning relationships between low-level actions and high-level task goals, allowing generalization across different action sequences that achieve the same outcome and improving robustness in long-horizon tasks [51, 66].
\end{itemize}

Progress in this area has been strongly driven by large-scale datasets such as UCF101 [70], which provide diverse and realistic in-the-wild videos for benchmarking action recognition models, as well as by methods that reduce annotation requirements through weakly supervised or unsupervised learning strategies.

\paragraph{Context Translation}
Context translation methods extend beyond visual appearance alignment and focus on transferring skills across varying tasks, environments, viewpoints, and object configurations. The main goal is to enable robots to generalize learned behaviors to new contexts by capturing the underlying structure of a task rather than relying on specific visual conditions.

Early approaches learn from paired demonstrations collected in different environments, where the same action is shown under varying contexts. These models learn to translate how a skill appears across settings, enabling robots to reproduce the same behavior in novel environments through reinforcement learning [133]. Later methods improve robustness by learning context-agnostic task representations that are combined with multi-modal inverse dynamics models. By integrating RGB and depth information, these systems achieve more stable action prediction across changes in viewpoint and object layout [134].

More recent approaches focus on scalability through unsupervised learning. The Learning by Watching (LbW) framework [121] combines video translation models with unsupervised keypoint extraction to map human demonstrations into robot domains without task-specific supervision. The resulting structured keypoint representations are then used as inputs for reinforcement learning, enabling imitation under varying contextual conditions while reducing the need for expert-labeled data.









\subsection{Multi-Modal Models}

Multi-modal models jointly learn from multiple data modalities, which can include text, images, videos, robot observations, and robot actions. 
Unlike specialized vision-language models, these approaches aim to learn a unified sequence model that can process heterogeneous modalities within a shared architecture.

\paragraph{Methods.}

Most multi-modal models are trained using autoregressive next-token prediction objectives over interleaved sequences of tokens from different modalities. 
Depending on the model, tokens may represent text, image patches, video frames, proprioceptive robot observations, or robot actions. 
The objective is to predict the next token in the sequence conditioned on the previous context:

\begin{equation}
p(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
\end{equation}

This training paradigm allows the model to learn cross-modal relationships between perception, language, and control.

Examples include the :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0} architecture, which jointly processes text, images, and robot actions, the humanoid locomotion model proposed by :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1} et al., which is trained on both action-labelled robot data and pose trajectories extracted from YouTube videos, and the 8-billion-parameter any-to-any transformer introduced by :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2} et al., which is trained on text, images, videos, and robot sensorimotor data.

These approaches are attractive due to their scalability and simplicity, since the same sequence modeling objective can be applied across many modalities and tasks.

\paragraph{Relevance for Robotics.}

Multi-modal models are particularly relevant for robotics because they learn a shared representation space that connects visual perception, language understanding, and robot control. 
By jointly training on robot data together with large-scale web data, the model can transfer semantic knowledge from vision-language tasks to robotic manipulation and decision making.

Furthermore these models can serve as strong initialization policies for reinforcement learning. 
A common strategy is to first pretrain the model using supervised sequence prediction on large-scale multi-modal datasets, and subsequently fine-tune the policy using reinforcement learning with task-specific rewards. 
This often improves sample efficiency and generalization compared to training reinforcement learning policies from scratch.

Even when the action prediction head is discarded, the learned encoder can still provide transferable cross-modal representations for downstream robotic tasks. 
In this sense, multi-modal models can also act as general-purpose representation backbones for imitation learning and reinforcement learning.

\paragraph{Relation to Vision-Language-Action Models.}

Multi-modal models that explicitly include robot actions are often referred to as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. 
However, the distinction between both categories is not always strict. 
VLA models typically focus on direct policy inference, where robot actions are predicted from visual observations and language instructions. 
In contrast, many multi-modal sequence models are designed as general-purpose token predictors that model arbitrary modality sequences. 

In practice, the same underlying architecture can often be used both as a general multi-modal foundation model and as a robotic control policy.














\paragraph{Structured and Hierarchical RL}

Structured and hierarchical reinforcement learning approaches aim to improve scalability and generalization by decomposing complex tasks into reusable subtasks, skills, or high-level goals. Instead of learning a single end-to-end policy, these methods separate high-level decision making from low-level motor control, enabling more efficient learning for long-horizon manipulation tasks.

Video data provides a natural source for discovering task structure and hierarchical behaviour. Human demonstrations often contain meaningful temporal abstractions, such as subtasks, intermediate goals, and reusable motion primitives, which can be extracted and used to guide policy learning. In hierarchical RL, high-level policies typically predict latent skills, subgoals, or task embeddings, while lower-level controllers execute the corresponding robot actions.

Several approaches learn structured latent representations directly from video demonstrations. Neural Task Programming (NTP) \cite{139} decomposes manipulation tasks into hierarchical compositions of primitive operations parameterized by demonstrations or trajectories, enabling rapid adaptation across tasks through meta-learning. Similarly, Adversarial Skill Networks (ASN) \cite{140} learn task-agnostic skill embeddings from unlabeled observations using adversarial and metric learning objectives, allowing reusable behaviours to emerge without explicit action supervision.

More recent methods condition reinforcement learning policies directly on video representations. Video-conditioned Policy Learning (ViP) \cite{141}, for example, learns high-level video embeddings from large-scale human activity datasets and uses these embeddings to condition downstream robot policies. During inference, relevant task representations can be retrieved from a library of demonstrations and used to guide policy execution, enabling multi-task learning and zero-shot generalization.

By incorporating hierarchical structure and high-level video representations, these methods improve exploration efficiency, facilitate long-horizon reasoning, and enable the reuse of learned skills across tasks. However, they also introduce additional challenges, including hierarchical credit assignment, stable multi-level training, and the need for large and diverse demonstration datasets.















\subsection{Dynamics Models}

A key advantage of dynamics-model-based approaches is their ability to learn useful world knowledge even from non-expert or unlabelled internet videos, making them more scalable than directly learning policies from video data alone. In particular, large pretrained video diffusion models have demonstrated promising generalization and long-horizon reasoning capabilities. Future research directions include hierarchical world models, integrating analytic simulators with learned video predictors, and generating synthetic robotic training data through large-scale video generation models.





















\subsection{Large-Scale VLA Models}

Multi-modal robotic learning aims to integrate several modalities, including visual observations, language instructions, robot states, tactile information, and actions, into a unified learning framework. The main objective is to improve task understanding, robustness, and generalization across diverse environments and manipulation tasks. These approaches are considered an important step toward general-purpose robotic intelligence.

A major class of such systems are Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which jointly model visual perception, language understanding, and robotic control. VLA models extend vision-language foundation models by incorporating robot actions as an additional modality, enabling end-to-end policy learning from multimodal observations and natural language instructions. Their goal is to learn general-purpose robot policies capable of executing complex tasks across different environments.

Modern VLA systems typically combine large visual backbones, large language models, and action prediction modules. Early approaches represented robot actions as discrete tokens and directly adapted pretrained vision-language models for robotic control. More recent methods instead model continuous actions and temporal dependencies using diffusion or flow-based generative architectures, enabling smoother and more expressive robot behavior.

Conceptually, current VLA approaches can be grouped into three main categories. The first category directly adapts pretrained vision-language models for robotic control. The second separates high-level semantic reasoning from low-level action generation through hierarchical architectures. The third focuses on large-scale pretraining on internet videos, simulations, and robot demonstrations to learn general physical dynamics before specializing to robotic tasks. These approaches often follow a ``data pyramid'' strategy that combines heterogeneous data sources to overcome the limited availability of robot demonstrations.

Overall, recent progress suggests that scalable robotic intelligence requires both strong multimodal perception and expressive continuous action modeling, forming the foundation for future general-purpose robotic systems.
